
# Actions and Dice Rolls
Characters drive the action in SWN, and when your character wishes to do something in the world, the GM will ask them to make an ability check to try to carry it out. 

:::{admonition} Rolling Mechanics
The rolling mechanic in SWN is a bit unique: You roll a certain number of die of the type associated with a given skill, and then sum the highest two, or lowest two (depending on if you have disadvantage or advantage):

- For a standard roll, roll 3DN and keep the highest two.
-  For a minor advantage roll, roll 4DN and keep the highest two.
- For a major advantaged roll, roll 5DN and keep the highest two.
- For a minor disadvantaged roll, roll 4DN and keep the lowest 2.
- For a major disadvantaged roll, roll 5DN and keep the lowest 2.
:::

## Risk Rolls
Sometimes, a player wants to attempt something far beyond their typical ability in a certain category. While this is normally not possible, a player may choose to expend a grit point in order to make a Risk Roll. A Risk Roll uses a d20 die instead of whichever die is typically associated with the skill check being made. The typical rules for critical success and failure still apply, meaning that a risk roll is both more likely to succeed, but also (potentially) more likely to fail critically than a standard attempt at the roll. Grit points are discussed later in this chapter.

## Exploding Rolls and Fate Rolls
When you roll the highest value on a die among your kept dice, it explodes! You reroll that dice, adding the new roll to your total, until it does not land on the maximum value. In principle, both of your kept dice could explode, and you would follow this procedure for both.

On the other hand, if you roll snake eyes (2 ones) on ANY of the dice in your original roll (including ones you normally remove), you feel the swirling strands of fate tighten around you, and time stills in a moment of unrealized potential. When this happens, you make a Fate Roll, which like Risk rolls uses a d20 dice. The table below lays out how to resolve the outcome of this roll.

Fate rolls grow more common the more dice are rolled (both in cases of advantage and disadvantage); Fate does not like odds being twisted, even when it is for good reason. On the other hand, as your level up your dice toward d12s, and hone expertise in your abilities, the chances of snake eyes decreases. Once fate steps in, however, it is out of your hands... almost. Several abilities in the game, scattered throughout the skill trees, grant circumstantial abilities to intervene in fateful moments, either for yourself or for your companions, to nudge a fated roll towards a positive outcome.




\begin{rpg-table}
   	\textbf{Fate Roll}  & \textbf{Outcome} \\
   	10-19  & The tension fizzles, and your ability check resolves as normal. \\
   	2-9  & Fate drifts away from you. If your ability check was a success, your GM will describe or ask you to describe an unintended negative consequence or outcome that accompanies it. If your check was a failure, an additional, meaningful consequence or outcome is triggered. \\
      Natural 1 & On a natural 1, fate abandons you. Regardless of the outcome of your original roll, you fail your check and a major consequence emerges.\\
      Natural 20 & Fate bestows you a boon. If your original roll would have failed the DL for the attempted check, it instead succeeds. And if your roll was already a success, a major positive consequence or outcome accompanies it, which your GM will describe or ask you to describe. \\
\end{rpg-table}



## Circumstantial Rolls
The six core skills described above are subdivided into various sub disciplines, which describe more specific circumstances for that skill --- for example, Perception/Sight, vs. Perception/Sound. These are detailed in Chapter 2. If a subdiscipline is not relevant, the GM will ask for a core skill roll. If the GM asks for a circumstantial roll (i.e., one of the subdisciplines), your default is to still simply use your die for the associated core skill. In practice, however, each player will have a unique set of abilities that provide bonuses only to certain circumstances (e.g., a bonus to Perception/Sound but not Perception/Sight). Your character sheet, or ability cards, will help you track your set of bonuses you may have across the sub disciplines, making it easy to know what to add to your roll when the situation calls for it. 

:::{admonition} Example
As an example, let's say you have a d6 die in the Perception core skill, but you have an origin ability that gives you +2 to Perception/Smell (e.g., you are a werewolf). For other Perception checks, like Perception/Sight, or Perception/Investigation, you will simply roll your standard check. But for Perception/Smell specifically, you will add an additional 2 to your total. 
:::





## Difficulty Level 
Every action proposed by a player that is nontrivial has an associated Difficulty Level (DL), which encompasses how difficult it is to achieve.
By rolling dice in addition to a base stat, the final roll is compared to the DL. In general, exceeding the DL by 5 is worth an additional benefit, and missing the DL by 10 is worth an additional drawback, though this is left to the GM's discretion.

The following Difficulty scale is a rough guideline for the DL of a task:
| DL Range | General Difficulty |
| --- | --- |
| 1-5 | Patently achievable by a commoner/untrained individual  |
| 6-11 | Relatively easy for a trained individual, but a challenge for one untrained. E.g., robustly striking a training dummy with a sword, or scaling a rocky wall | 
| 12-20 | | 


\begin{rpg-table}
   	\textbf{DL Range}  & \textbf{General Difficulty} \\
   	1-5  & Patently achievable by a commoner/untrained individual \\
   	6-11  & Relatively easy for a trained individual, but a challenge for one untrained. E.g., robustly striking a training dummy with a sword, or scaling a rocky wall \\
      12-20 & A robust but achievable task for a trained individual, nearly impossible for an untrained one. E.g., disarming another trained swordsman's sword, or lying to a trained spy\\
      20-25 & A near-impossible task for anyone not specifically trained in a sub-discipline, reasonable but challenging for one trained. E.g., climbing a cliffside, or spotting a hidden figure from hundreds of feet away. \\
      25-30 & The hardest tasks reasonably achievable by one highly trained in a skill. Perfect execution by such an individual. E.g., a quad spin by a world class ice skater \\
      30+ & By default, should be “impossible”, though with heroics, who knows?
\end{rpg-table}

### What is an Action?
When your character does something discrete in the world, it is generally considered an action for the purposes of these rules and mechanics, whether it requires a skill roll or not. Key exceptions include making Perception, Personality, and Reasoning based skill checks, which are considered free actions. 

Some actions require checks, some cost stamina.

## Movement
You can move (on land) a number of feet per round given by your Physicality Die based on the table below. Movement can be broken up at will into chunks with actions taken in between.
\begin{rpg-table}
   	\textbf{Physicality Die}  & \textbf{Movement/round} \\
   	d4  & 30 ft \\
   	d6  & 35 ft \\
   	d8 & 40 ft \\ 
        d10 &  45 ft \\ 
        d12 &  50 ft \\ 
\end{rpg-table}

Any non-standard movement type --- Swimming, Climbing, Jumping, etc. --- have half the value for base movement in the table above. These are also circumstantial Physicality rolls, and can be invested in to gain bonuses to checks made to do them. Swimming through a calm pond does not require a swimming check, for example, but navigating rapids might. Or, if an element if your backstory is that you are deathly afraid of heights, a simple staircase (suspended over a cliff) might invoke a climbing skill check, while for your party mate it may not.
